# The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs

*Carmine Gallo — via Founders podcast #350 (David Senra)*

Premise: Jobs treated keynotes as a competitive weapon. Business is sales — you're
selling when you pitch, when you hire, when you raise, when you talk to a customer.
"A person can have the greatest idea in the world, but if that person can't convince
enough other people, it doesn't matter."

---

## 1. What are you really selling?

**You are not selling the product. You are selling the improvement.**

Jobs didn't sell computers; he sold "tools that unleash human potential." The one
question that matters to the audience is **"why should I care?"** — i.e. *what does
this do for me?*

> "You've got to start with the customer experience and work back towards the
> technology — not the other way around." — Jobs, 1997

### The structure Jobs used (iMac, 1998)

1. **State the customer's goal first, before the product exists in the room.**
   "We made this for the number one reason customers tell us they want a computer:
   to get on the internet simply and fast."
2. **Name the problem with the alternatives, in blunt words.**
   Last year's processor. "Pretty crummy displays." "These things are *ugly*."
3. **Draw the verbal road map — say what that means.**
   "What that means is they have lower performance and they are harder to use."
4. **Then, and only then, the product as the solution.**
   "The new iMac is fast — it *screams*." "The new iMac has a *gorgeous* display."

### Examples of the same move

- **Juan Trippe / Pan Am → United Fruit:** paperwork had to cross a 9,000ft mountain,
  3 days by dangerous road. His pitch: *1½ hours by air.* A 48x improvement, stated
  as the customer's saving, not as an aviation capability.
- **Ivar Kreuger:** "Government loans for match monopolies." One phrase, repeatable
  by investors to other investors — which is why it spread.
- **Edwin Land → American Optical:** rented a hotel room facing the sun, put a bowl
  of goldfish on the sill so glare flooded the room, apologised that they "probably
  can't even see the fish," then handed each exec a polarising filter. Demo > claim.
- **iPod:** "1,000 songs in your pocket." Not spec, not category — the better future,
  written from the customer's side.
- **Moses Kagan, on raising from non-professional investors:** they don't know MOIC,
  IRR or loss-to-lease. Include a slide titled **"What we will do with your money"**
  in plain English.

### The rule underneath it

> "Advertising which promises no benefit to the customer does not sell, yet the
> majority of campaigns contain no promise whatsoever." — David Ogilvy

Products that struggle are usually a solution in search of a problem.

### Then: one line, repeated relentlessly

Compress the main idea to one sentence, then repeat it everywhere — keynote, slides,
press release, website, brochure, conversation. MacBook Air was launched as "the
world's thinnest notebook" **seven times** across one announcement cycle.

*Repetition is persuasive. Human nature has a flaw: we forget that we forget.*

## 2. How he actually built the presentations

- **Plan in analog.** Pen, paper, whiteboard — before opening Keynote. Jobs acted as
  his own slideshow, jumping up mid-conversation to draw (Rick Gerson's account of
  the Pixar pitch: a hub-and-spoke of every business you could launch off one film).
- **Structure:**
  1. List everything you want the audience to remember.
  2. Cut until **three** main points remain.
  3. Under each, add stories, easy-to-remember facts, metaphors, social proof.
- **The rule of three** is the most powerful concept in communication, because
  short-term memory is tiny. Stanford commencement = opening, three stories,
  conclusion. Transitions stated flatly: *"My second story is about love and loss."*
- **Don't overstuff.** "Most people giving a pitch try to squeeze 2 megabytes of data
  into a pipe that carries 128 kilobytes."
- **Start with the problem, not the product.** The most common founder-pitch failure.
- **Slides carry images, almost no words.** You can't read a photo, so you must know
  the message cold — the result is that you *speak to* the audience rather than
  *read to* them.
- **Give every number context.**
  - "Our market share is greater than BMW or Mercedes in the car industry" — and
    nobody thinks *they're* disadvantaged.
  - 4 million iPhones in 200 days → **20,000 a day**.
  - $1m in $100 bills = a 16-inch stack. $1bn = taller than the Empire State
    Building. A million seconds ago = 11½ days. A billion seconds ago = 31 years.
- **Use uncommon words — uncommon, not complex.** *crummy, ugly, it screams,
  gorgeous, amazing, insanely great, so good you'll want to lick them, bitching.*
  Audiences want to be informed **and entertained** (see Munger's zingers). No jargon.
- **Social proof.** Reading favourable reviews aloud on stage was a standing
  technique. Munger: social proof gives huge advantages to scale.
- **Practice.** Best presenters run ~90 hours of rehearsal per hour on stage. Jobs
  was involved in every detail — which is exactly why it looked effortless.
  "Every slide was written like a piece of poetry."

## 3. Develop a messianic sense of purpose

Passion is infectious; belief is irresistible. It transfers.

- "We've got some incredible ideas that will revolutionise the way people use
  computers. Apple is going to be the most important computer company in the world."
- "The people who buy them are the creative spirits in this world… they're out to
  change the world. We make tools for these kinds of people. A lot of times people
  think they're crazy, but in that craziness we see genius."
- Lee Clow: "From the time he was a kid, Steve thought his products could change the
  world. That's the key to understanding him."

**Phil Knight, *Shoe Dog*:** couldn't sell encyclopedias, was mediocre at mutual
funds and "felt dead inside" — then couldn't write orders fast enough for running
shoes. *"Why was selling shoes so different? Because I wasn't selling. I believed in
running."*

**Ogilvy:** "I admire people who work with gusto. If you don't enjoy what you're
doing, I beg you to find another job."

**Peter Thiel's definition, which Jobs lived:** properly understood, any new and
better way of doing things is technology.

**The closing demo — the bicycle for the mind:** humans rank unimpressively for
efficiency of locomotion, about a third down the list. A human *on a bicycle* blows
the condor off the top of the chart. "That is what a computer is to me — the most
remarkable tool we've ever come up with. It's the equivalent of a bicycle for our
mind." Note the shape: a study, a surprising fact, a reversal, then the metaphor.

## Checklist for the next thing you pitch or write

- [ ] What improvement am I selling — stated as the customer's better future?
- [ ] Is the problem stated *before* the product appears?
- [ ] Is there one headline sentence, and does it answer "why should I care?"
- [ ] Am I repeating that headline everywhere, verbatim?
- [ ] Three points. Not five, not nine.
- [ ] Every number given a human-scale comparison.
- [ ] Plain, vivid words. Zero jargon.
- [ ] A demo, a story, or a photo instead of a bullet list.
- [ ] Social proof somewhere.
- [ ] Rehearsed enough that it looks unrehearsed.
